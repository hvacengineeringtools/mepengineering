Stack the layers from outside to inside, add the air films, and the sheet gives the assembly U-factor and R-value, shows where the resistance sits, and checks it against the 90.1 maximum U-factor and minimum continuous-insulation R for the climate zone. It handles series layers only: insulation that is continuous, with no framing in parallel. · ·

          Checked against the standard

          The LayerStack example (above-deck roof, zone 4, nonresidential) was checked line by line against the 90.1-2025 (SI) PDF:

          • Limit. Table 5.5-4 (p. 56): insulation entirely above deck, nonresidential, U-0.184 and R-5.3 c.i. The example’s limit matches.
          • Films. A9.4.1 and A2.2.1: R 0.03 exterior and R 0.11 interior, heat flow up. Both match.
          • Metal deck. Table A9.4.4-1 and A2.2.1: R 0. Matches.
          • Built-up roofing. Table A9.4.4-1 lists R 0.06 for 9.5 mm. The example showed 10 mm; this sheet uses 9.5 mm. The R is unchanged.
          • Insulation. The conductivity of 0.025 W/(m·K) for polyisocyanurate is not a 90.1 value; it is a typical product figure and should be replaced by the maker’s rated value. 135 mm at k 0.025 gives R 5.40, above the R-5.3 minimum.
          • Result. R 0.03 + 0.06 + 5.40 + 0 + 0.11 = 5.60, U = 0.179 W/(m²·K), below 0.184. The Appendix A table for roofs with insulation entirely above deck (Table A2.2.3, p. 275) gives U-0.18 for R-5.3 insulation alone; the same series sum for R-5.3 is 1 ÷ 5.44 = 0.184.

          While doing this check, sheet XXV’s zone 4 to 7 values (and some of zone 8’s fenestration values) were found not to match the standard and have been replaced with the 2025 table values.

          Two ways to comply

          Section 5.5 lets the assembly comply either by the maximum assembly U-factor or by the minimum rated R-value of insulation. The sheet shows both. Where an assembly has framing in parallel with the insulation (steel or wood framing, for example), the R-value method does not give the true U-factor and the standard says to use the maximum U-factor.

          Points to note

          • Floor film. A5.2.1 (p. 296) says R 0.16 for the interior film, heat flow down, but A9.4.1 gives R 0.17. The sheet offers 0.17, the A9.4.1 value; the difference to the U-factor of an insulated floor is under 0.2 %.
          • Mass wall base assembly. A3.1.1 (p. 279) assumes R 0.03 and R 0.12 films, 200 mm concrete, and R 0.08 for 13 mm gypsum board on insulated walls.
          • Rated R. Insulation R-values are rated at a mean temperature of 24 °C by the maker’s test; a conductivity taken from a datasheet at 10 °C will be a little better than the rated figure.
          • Not covered. Framed walls and roofs, metal buildings, attics, below-grade walls, slabs, thermal bridging at fasteners, and ventilated air spaces.
          • Material list. Only materials with a value in the standard are listed as fixed layers. Insulation and any other product need a value from the maker.

          Air spaces

          • Method. The “Air space, unventilated” layer gives R by the calculation in EN ISO 6946 Annex B. It depends on the thickness, the direction of heat flow and the emittance of the two faces. The result is not a 90.1 table value.
          • Check. For ordinary surfaces the calculation reproduces the printed ISO 6946 table to within its rounding. For a 13 mm space it gives 0.16 m²·K/W; the ASHRAE Handbook – Fundamentals value for a 13 mm space bounded by building materials is 0.90 ft²·h·°F/Btu, which is 0.158 m²·K/W.
          • Thickness. R rises quickly up to about 25 mm and then hardly changes. Above 25 mm a wider space does not insulate much more, because the air moves. Below 5 mm the space is easily bridged.
          • Choices. “Ordinary faces” means building surfaces of emittance 0.9 on both sides. “One face foil” and “Both faces foil” mean bright aluminium foil of emittance 0.05 on one or both faces. Horizontal heat flow is for a wall, upward for a roof or ceiling in winter, downward for a floor in winter.
          • Direction. A downward heat flow (a floor in winter) has the highest R, because warm air stays on top. An upward flow (a roof or ceiling in winter) has the lowest.
          • Bright foil. It cuts the radiant part of the heat flow, so one bright face can more than triple R. The foil must face the air space and stay bright. Dust and condensation raise its emittance and the benefit is lost over time.
          • Conditions. The space must be sealed and unventilated, with width and height at least ten times its thickness and no air leakage, and with no bridging. A cavity with open vents is a ventilated space. ISO 6946 treats a well-ventilated space by leaving out the air space and every layer outside it.
          • Compliance. 90.1 Appendix A lists its own air-space values (Section A9.4.2). If the assembly is to be submitted for compliance, use the standard’s value, entered as an “Other layer”, and compare it with this calculation.
