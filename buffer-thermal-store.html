Thermal storage and buffer vessels. Three related jobs for a heating or chilled-water circuit. A buffer vessel adds water to the circuit so that a heat pump or chiller does not start and stop too often. A thermal store holds heating or cooling energy for a set time, for defrost, load shifting or load lopping. The tank size turns a required volume into a diameter and height. Fill in only the sections you need; the volume from section 2 or 3 can be carried into section 4. This sheet does not size the feed and expansion tank of an open system: use sheet V, Feed and expansion tank for that.

          Which one do I need?

          • Buffer vessel: the plant is larger than the load at times, so it cycles on and off. Extra water slows the temperature swings and lets the compressor run for longer each time. Size it from the smallest compressor step and the starts per hour the maker allows.
          • Thermal store: the load is supplied from stored energy for a set time, for example while the heat pump defrosts. Size it from the output, the time and the temperature difference.
          • Tank size: turns either volume into a diameter and height.

          How reliable is the method?

          The arithmetic of the guide’s examples is reproduced in section 6. The points below are about whether the method suits your case.

          • Buffer vessel: it is a rule of thumb. The guide asks for enough water that the circuit’s flow takes one cycle time to pass through. The usual formula uses the temperature band of the controller instead: V = Q × t ÷ (4 ρ c ΔTband), which gives the worst case at half load. The two agree only when the band is a quarter of the circuit ΔT, which is 1.25 K for a 5 K circuit. A band narrower than that needs more water than the guide gives, and a wider band needs less. Section 2 shows both, using the band you enter.
          • The water that counts. At low load, zone valves and terminal units close and part of the circuit stops circulating. Only the water that keeps passing through the unit helps. Count it conservatively.
          • The 10 to 35 litres per kW range is a sanity band, not a limit. Published guidance differs by plant type and by temperature, so use the plant maker’s minimum water volume where there is one. The guide says the same.
          • Smallest step. The method assumes the smallest compressor on the smallest unit is the worst case. If the plant can unload below that step, the result is not the worst case.
          • Thermal store: it is an energy balance. V = Q × t ÷ (ρ c ΔT) is exact for water that stays at its design temperatures. In a real tank some water mixes across the thermocline, so not all of it is usable. That is why section 3 has a usable-share box and a margin.
          • The temperature difference must be the one the store really works between. A small error in ΔT is a large error in volume: halving ΔT doubles the volume.
          • Properties. The guide’s 4.21 kJ/kg·K and 999.8 kg/m³ suit cold water. At 40 °C the real values are about 4.18 and 992, which makes the volume about 1.5 % larger. The margin covers this, but the sheet lets you choose the values at the mean temperature. Glycol needs its own values.
          • The store is sized for discharge only. Check that the plant can recharge it in the time between demands.
          • Tank geometry is an ideal cylinder. Supports, dished ends, connections, insulation and access space add to the real vessel.

          What is not included

          Pressure rating and expansion of the vessel, heat loss from the tank, charging time and plant capacity, diffusers and internal stratification devices, pump and pipe losses, and the manufacturer’s tank database.
