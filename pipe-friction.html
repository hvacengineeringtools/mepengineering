Gives the velocity, Reynolds number, friction factor and pressure drop per metre for water (or any liquid you give the density and viscosity of) in a round pipe. The flow can be entered, or worked out from a load and a temperature difference with an optional safety factor. The pipe is chosen from the British or Australian tube tables (copper BS EN 1057 or AS 1432, steel BS EN 10255 or AS 1074, and BS EN 10216/10217 steel with a wall of your choice), or typed in. The pipework specification you supplied is in section 3, and the sheet can check your pipe against it. The pressure limit is yours: leave it blank and nothing is judged against a limit. The one notice that always shows is for a velocity outside what the friction formula covers. Every step of the working is in the side bar.

                Fluid properties

                What the sheet does

                It finds the pressure lost to friction in a straight length of round pipe running full of liquid. The loss is given per metre of pipe, and over a length if you enter one. The method is the Darcy–Weisbach equation, with the friction factor from the Altshul–Tsal formula. Flow is either entered or worked out from a load, a temperature difference and a specific heat.

                Choices left to you

                • The flow safety factor, the specific heat, the temperature difference and the pressure limit are all yours. None is fixed in the sheet. A blank limit means no limit; a blank safety factor box (unticked) means none is added.
                • No limit on velocity is applied. The only velocity notice is for a velocity where the friction formula itself does not hold, and it stays on the sheet whenever it applies.
                • The pipe is yours to pick: copper to BS EN 1057 or AS 1432, steel to BS EN 10255 or AS 1074, BS EN 10216/10217 steel with a wall you choose, the workbook’s own sizes, or any outside diameter, wall or inside diameter you type. Roughness, density and viscosity can all be typed over too.

                What the friction formula covers

                • The Altshul–Tsal formula is an explicit approximation to the Colebrook equation for turbulent flow. The sheet treats it as valid from Reynolds number 4000 up to 10⁸, and for relative roughness ε/D up to 0.05 (the range of the Moody chart). Below Reynolds number 2300 the flow is laminar and f = 64/Re applies instead; between 2300 and 4000 the flow is transitional and no friction factor is reliable.
                • These limits are the usual ones for this kind of formula. They are not taken from a particular clause, so use your own where a standard gives different ones.
                • I checked the formula against the full Colebrook equation for sizes DN25 to DN450 at 0.5, 1.5 and 3 m/s. The pressure drop agreed within about 3 %, and within 1 % for most of the steel sizes.

                Source and standing

                • Darcy–Weisbach is the standard friction-loss equation behind pipe sizing guidance generally.
                • The Altshul–Tsal friction factor is published in the ASHRAE Handbook, Fundamentals, duct design chapter (the 2001 edition, chapter 34, is the one cited by the sources I could read). It is written there for ducts. Applying it to water pipes is mathematically sound, but it is not the form a pipe chapter prints, which I understand to be Colebrook–White. I could not open the CIBSE Guide C or the ASHRAE pipe chapter to confirm.
                • The method was extracted from a pipe sizing workbook (the “Pipetulator” sheet of a project calculation), whose copper sizes are Australian (AS 1432 Type B). Its fixed velocity limits and fixed pressure limits are not carried over.

                What the British Standards cover

                • Copper, BS EN 1057. Round copper tube for water, gas and heating. The supplier tables I read run from 6 mm to 267 mm outside diameter, in half-hard (R250) and hard (R290) temper, with annealed coils (R220) in the small sizes. Wall thickness is set per size in two common series, Table X (thinner) and Table Y (thicker). The sheet lists 15 mm to 267 mm. Table Y is not listed above 108 mm in the tables I read.
                • Steel, BS EN 10255 (it replaced BS 1387). Threaded or welded steel tube, outside diameter 10.2 mm to 165.1 mm, which is DN6 to DN150 (1/8 in to 6 in). Light, medium and heavy walls. Light stops at DN100. The sheet lists DN8 to DN150. For DN8 and DN10 the table I read gave only one outside diameter (the maximum), so those two inside diameters are slightly small, which errs on the high side for pressure drop.
                • Steel to BS EN 10216 and 10217. BS EN 10255 stops at DN150. Above that, steel pipe for pressure services is made to BS EN 10216 (seamless) or BS EN 10217 (welded), with outside diameters from BS EN 10220. The pipework specification also names them for hot water from DN50. Those standards do not fix one wall for each size: the buyer chooses it. So for DN50 to DN450 the sheet gives the outside diameter and you pick the wall.
                • These are product standards. They fix the tube dimensions and quality. They do not set a velocity or pressure-drop limit; that comes from your brief or the design guide you use.
                • I could not open the standards, which are paid documents. The dimensions come from supplier and reference tables that agree with each other, and the sheet lets you type any outside diameter, wall or inside diameter to override them. Check them against the standard before you issue anything.
                • For BS EN 10255 the outside diameter used is the middle of the maximum and minimum in the table, so the inside diameter is nominal; the tolerance on the wall is not applied.

                The Australian standards, and what the workbook used

                • Copper, AS 1432, Copper tubes for plumbing, gasfitting and drainage applications. Round seamless tube. Types A and B run from DN6 to DN200, Type C from DN10 to DN25 and Type D from DN32 to DN150, with Type A the heaviest wall and Type D the lightest. Nominal sizes follow the old inch outside diameters (DN15 is 12.70 mm, DN20 is 19.05 mm, DN25 is 25.40 mm). Editions of 1990, 1996 and 2004 are listed by the sellers, so check which one your project cites. The sheet lists DN15 to DN200.
                • Steel, AS 1074-1989 (R2018), Steel tubes and tubulars for ordinary service. Threaded tube, DN8 to DN150, in light, medium and heavy walls. In the ACADS-BSG table its inside diameters agree with the BS EN 10255 figures within about 0.1 mm, apart from DN80 heavy (79.1 mm in that table against 78.75 mm worked out here), so the sheet uses the same outside diameters and walls for both. The comparison is in the Pipe and fluid data tab. The sheet lists DN15 to DN150.
                • Larger steel water pipe, AS 1579-2001 (R2018), Arc welded steel pipes and fittings for water and waste water. It covers DN100 and larger, for water and waste water up to 6.8 MPa. I could not find its dimension tables, so the sheet does not list AS 1579 sizes: use the BS EN 10216/10217 choice, or type the outside diameter and wall from your supplier’s table.
                • What the workbook used. Its copper inside diameters match the ACADS-BSG table “Australian copper, AS 1432 Type B” (ten of the eleven sizes to 0.01 mm), and match Type B worked out from the outside diameter and wall within 0.15 mm. So the workbook copper is AS 1432 Type B, not BS EN 1057. Its steel sizes DN200 to DN450 are ASME B36.10M standard-weight pipe (outside diameters 219.1 to 457 mm, walls 8.18 and 9.27 mm for DN200 and DN250 and 9.53 mm above), agreeing with the standard-weight inside diameters within 0.25 mm; the comparison is in the Pipe and fluid data tab. The ACADS-BSG table labels those rows “American steel”. That is not an Australian standard table. BS EN 10216 and 10217 tube is also made to these sizes with a wall chosen by the buyer, so the figures are acceptable there too. The workbook steel is kept under “Workbook sizes as supplied”.
                • The copper DN50 inside diameter, 48.3 mm, is correct for AS 1432 Type B (50.80 mm outside, 1.22 mm wall). I earlier took it for a mis-entry. That was wrong.
                • I could not open AS 1432 or AS 1074, which are paid documents. The outside diameters and walls for AS 1432 are from Austral Wright Metals’ tube list, and the inside diameters from the ACADS-BSG help page. Both are secondary sources; check them against the standard before you issue anything.
                • Like the British tubes, these are product standards. They fix dimensions and quality, not a velocity or pressure-drop limit.

                Data to check

                • Roughness is chosen on the sheet: from the pipe type (0.0015 mm copper, 0.046 mm steel as in the workbook), commercial steel 0.046 mm, galvanised iron 0.15 mm, drawn tubing 0.0015 mm, or your own. The published values carry real uncertainty; the source suggests allowing 30 % to 50 % on typical figures. Ageing and scale are not included, and an old pipe can be much rougher.
                • The workbook’s three fluids (about 10, 30 and 40 °C) agree with the water tables to within 0.1 % in density and 0.2 % in viscosity. The sheet can also take water at any temperature from 0 to 200 °C. It is for plain water: glycol or other mixtures need their own density and viscosity.
                • BS EN 1057 copper has thinner walls than AS 1432 Type B, so its inside diameters are larger. Choosing between them changes the result: the workbook copper gives up to about three times the pressure drop of Table X copper in the smallest sizes. Use the standard your project calls for.

                Where the data came from

                • Water density and kinematic viscosity by temperature: the Engineering ToolBox tables for water, which cover 0 to 360 °C along the saturation line. The sheet uses 0 to 200 °C.
                • Roughness: Engineers Edge’s table, which quotes the Hydraulic Institute and F. M. White (commercial steel 0.046 mm, new galvanised iron 0.15 mm, drawn tubing 0.0015 mm). I did not have CIBSE Guide C, which gives its own figures.
                • ASME B36.10M pipe: the Engineering ToolBox tables. The metric table gives DN15 to DN200 in standard weight, Schedule 40 and Schedule 80. For DN250 to DN450 the page gave inches only: standard weight from that table, and the Schedule 40 walls (10.31, 11.13, 12.70 and 14.27 mm for DN300 to DN450) are the ASME inch walls converted, cross-checked against a second table that rounds them to 0.41, 0.44, 0.50 and 0.56 in. Schedule 80 above DN200 is not in the sheet.
                • BS EN 10255 DN8 and DN10: the Engineering ToolBox BS 1387 table.
                • BS EN 10216/10217 outside diameters (60.3, 76.1, 88.9, 114.3, 139.7 and 168.3 mm for DN50 to DN150): the usual BS EN 10220 and ISO series, which I have not checked against a published table here. They match the ASME outside diameters for DN50, DN80, DN100 and DN150, and differ at DN65 (76.1 against 73.0 mm) and DN125 (139.7 against 141.3 mm).
                • All of these are secondary sources. The standards themselves are paid documents I could not open.

                Not included

                Fittings, valves, strainers, coils and elevation changes, the pump head, ageing of the pipe, and a flow that changes along the pipe. The sheet gives straight pipe friction only. Fittings and valves can be added by equivalent length or loss coefficient if wanted.
