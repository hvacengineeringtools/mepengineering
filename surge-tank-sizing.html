Sizes the gas-charged surge tank on a pump discharge line, by the maker’s published method (sizing sheet MC 10261, 03/19). Two cases are worked: water hammer protection when the pump starts, and cavitation protection when it stops. The tank must be at least as large as the bigger of the two. Enter the pump flow, the pipe, the line pressures and the pre-charge gas in either US units or SI units; the answer is given in your units. The maker’s own worked example can be loaded with one button and checked against the figures printed on the sheet.

2. Pump and pressures

Not sure what to enter? The pictures of the system, the tank and the pressure changes when the pump starts and stops are in the side panel.
  • P₂: the highest pressure the pipe, valves and tank may see. You set it.
  • Ps: the pressure in the line with the pump stopped (static head, or the pump’s set pressure).
  • P₁: the gas pressure the tank is charged to before it is put on line, with no water in it. It is a gauge pressure (0 = atmosphere). The maker’s rule sets it below Ps: 15 % below for the pump-start case and 50 % below for the pump-stop case. That rule is the only reason for the percentage boxes. If you already have a pre-charge pressure, type it in the pressure box instead and the percentage is ignored.
  • Pump starts: the water in the pipe is set moving and the pressure briefly rises above Ps (water hammer). The tank takes in water to keep the peak below P₂.
  • Pump stops: the water keeps moving for a moment and the pressure briefly falls below Ps, which can make vapour pockets form (cavitation). The tank pushes water back into the line to hold the pressure up. These are pressure swings in the pipe, not the pump’s cut-in and cut-out settings.
The pressure in the line with the pump stopped.
The highest pressure the pipe and fittings may see while the tank is working.
Pre-charge pressure P₁

P₁ is the pressure of the gas (air or nitrogen) that is charged into the tank before it is put on line, with no water in it. It is a gauge pressure: the reading on a gauge fitted to the tank’s gas valve, with 0 at the atmosphere. If a maker’s sheet gives an absolute pressure, subtract the atmosphere first (14.7 psi, 101.3 kPa or 1.013 bar).

Notes and formulae

Start here if you are not sure what the boxes mean. Pictures A to C show what you enter and what it refers to in the system and in the tank. The two diagrams below them show how the pressure in the pipe changes with time, step by step, when the pump starts and when it stops.

How the pressure changes in time

The levels P₂, Ps and P₁ are your own numbers. The timing and the size of the swings are drawn for illustration only: the method sizes the tank, it does not calculate a pressure–time curve.

The working with the values now on the sheet, in US units (the units of the maker’s formulae).

1. Pump starts: water hammer protection

r = (P₂ + 14.7) / (P₁ + 14.7), pressures in psi gauge
Tank volume = [ (SG × 2 × Qs × L / (449 × a)) × r1/n / (r1/n − 1) ] × 7.481 gallons
Qs in gal/min, L in ft, a in ft/s. 449 converts gal/min to ft³/s; 7.481 converts ft³ to gallons.

2. Pump stops: cavitation protection

Tc = 2 L / a, in seconds
Vv = Qss × Tc × 7.481, with Qss the pipe velocity in ft/s from the maker’s chart
AF = 1 − ((P₁ + 14.7) / (P₂ + 14.7))1/n
Tank volume = Vv / AF

3. Pipe velocity

Qss = Qs / (449 × A), A = π (d / 12)² / 4 in ft², d the inside diameter in inches

4. Units

1 gal/min = 0.0630902 L/s, 1 ft = 0.3048 m, 1 psi = 6.894757 kPa, 1 in = 25.4 mm, 1 US gal = 3.785412 L. The sheet converts SI inputs to US, works in US units as the maker does, and converts the tank volume back. Atmospheric pressure is taken as 14.7 psi, as on the maker’s sheet.

Gauge and absolute pressure

Every pressure you type (Ps, P₂, P₁) is a gauge pressure, as read on a pressure gauge: 0 is the atmosphere. Absolute pressure = gauge + atmosphere, with the atmosphere taken as 14.7 psi (101.3 kPa, 1.013 bar). Example: 100 psi gauge is 114.7 psi absolute, or 790.8 kPa absolute.

The sheet uses the absolute value inside the gas law, because the gas volume depends on absolute pressure: r = (P₂ + 14.7) / (P₁ + 14.7). Pipe, valve and tank ratings are also gauge pressures, so P₂ is typed as a gauge pressure too. A gauge on the tank’s gas valve, with no water in the tank, reads P₁.

Progress of the pressure change

Pre-charge pressure

The maker’s sheet pre-charges 15 % below the static pressure for the pump-start case and 50 % below it for the pump-stop case, and the sheet does the same by default. You can change the percentages or type the pressure.

A check on the pump-stop formula

The maker’s pump-stop formula multiplies the pipe velocity (ft/s) by a time, which gives a length. A volume would need the pipe area as well. Used as printed, the formula is the same as taking the pipe area as 1 ft². This sheet follows the printed formula, so its answer matches the maker’s example and tank selection. For comparison, the result shows what the pump-stop volume would be if the vacuum volume were taken as flow × Tc. For a 4 inch pipe that is about one eleventh of the printed figure. Ask the maker which basis their tank selection rests on before choosing a smaller tank.

Printing errors in the maker’s example

In the pump-stop example the sheet gives Vv = 83.70 and then divides 83.04 by 0.541 to get 153.5 gallons. Dividing 83.70 by 0.541 gives 154.7, and working it exactly gives 154.9. The chart value for a 12 inch pipe at 3,500 gpm (10.30 ft/s) is also out of line with the rest of its column; the others give 10.03.

What is not included

Pipe friction, branches, pump run-down time, valve closing, and the choice of tank model. The method treats the line as one straight pipe with one flow.
