Quick check: heat rejected, and what a change of water flow does

The short version. Enter the water flow, the water temperatures in and out and the wet-bulb to get the heat the tower rejects, the range and the approach. Tick the box under it to change the flow and see the effect. This block stands on its own and uses a typical tower. Sections 1 to 4 below are the full method, with the tower’s own characteristic and water use.

2. Tower characteristic: how C is found

How a tower’s performance changes with flow depends on its characteristic KaV/L against L/G, written KaV/L = C·(L/G)−m. It belongs to the fill, the tower type and its size, and it is not on any nameplate. Choose how to find C and m. Options 1 to 3 use the maker’s or measured data and are better. Option 4 is an estimate from one design point and is the default. Set this section once, at the design point; the tower rule in section 1 then keeps C and the air flow fixed while you change the load, flow or wet-bulb. Closed-circuit towers, dry or hybrid coolers and evaporative condensers do not follow the Merkel method and are not covered.

What to enter to get the air flow and L/G

  1. Air volume. Take it for the fan speed of the operating point. A fan at 80 % speed moves about 80 % of the air; multiply by the fan speed box.
  2. Dry-air density. The entering dry-bulb and the wet-bulb of section 1 fix the density (about 1.12 kg/m³ at 32 / 25.5 °C at sea level). Mass flow G = air volume × density.
  3. L/G = water flow ÷ G. A usual tower is 0.75 to 1.5. The sheet flags a value below 0.5 or above 2.5.
  4. Check the air leaving. The sheet gives the leaving air enthalpy and the saturated temperature it equals. It should be a few kelvin below the water in; a value at or above it means the air flow is too small for the range.
  5. Do this once, at the design point. Do not re-enter a new air flow for a different load. Change the load, flow or wet-bulb in section 1 or 3, and the fan speed in section 3, and the sheet scales the air for you.

          Tower method

          Range, approach, effectiveness, evaporation and blow-down are as in PDH course M374 (Primo), sections 6 to 8, 15 and 20. The tower characteristic is the Merkel equation evaluated with four points at 10, 40, 60 and 90 % of the range (the usual Cooling Technology Institute four-point integration). Air leaving the fill is taken at an enthalpy rise of (L/G)·cw·ΔT, as in the handbook, which neglects the small evaporation term. The figures were checked against the handbook’s worked examples 15-2 to 15-7 and agree within 0.02 °F.

          Existing tower in section 1

          With the box ticked, section 1 treats the tower as a fixed machine. Its characteristic (section 2) is frozen, with its air flow, at the operating point present when the box was ticked or the characteristic last changed. Only three boxes are then free inputs, because the tower itself decides the approach: approach = f(range, flow, wet-bulb). Change the flow while holding wet-bulb and heat rejected, and the approach, range and both water temperatures all move, matching the result of section 3. Untick the box for plain arithmetic between the boxes (R = H − C, A = C − W, Q = 4.186·flow·R), where holding the wet-bulb, approach and load fixes everything except range and flow.

          How C is found: the four options

          The line KaV/L = C·(L/G)−m is a sample for an open-circuit tower with fill. The slope and constant differ between film and splash fill, counterflow and crossflow, and between makers, so the sheet offers four ways to find them.

          • 1. Maker’s constants. Type C and m, or two points (L/G, KaV/L) read from the maker’s curve. The sheet then finds the L/G at which section 1 lies on the curve. No air flow is needed.
          • 2. Two or more of the maker’s selection points. Ask the maker for the same tower selected at two or three different water flows. For each, enter the water flow, water in, water out and wet-bulb, and give the fan air flow once. The sheet finds KaV/L at each point with the Merkel integral, takes L/G = water flow ÷ dry-air flow, and fits the line through the points. Two points give C and m exactly; three or four give a least-squares line and show how far each point is from it. The fan is assumed to be at the same speed at every point.
          • 3. Test or site measurement (CTI ATC-105 style). The same fit, but the dry-bulb and fan air flow are entered for each point, so fan speed can differ between tests. A single test gives KaV/L at one L/G, and m must then be assumed. Measurement error moves the result: the fit should use readings at steady load, with the wet-bulb measured at the tower inlet.
          • 4. One design point and an assumed slope (the default). The sheet takes section 1 as the design point, finds L/G from the air flow (or uses an assumed L/G if you have no air data), and calculates KaV/L there. C follows from the assumed m. It is an estimate, and the further the new case is from the design point, the more the assumed slope matters.

          Closed-circuit towers, dry and hybrid coolers and evaporative condensers have other physics and are not covered by this sheet.

          Air flow and L/G

          In options 2 and 4 the air flow is entered at the fan speed of the operating point, in one of three ways: the maker’s air volume; the number of fans, the fan diameter and the average air velocity; or the fan shaft power, total pressure and total efficiency. The last is rough and is best used as a check on the first. The sheet takes the dry-air density from the entering dry-bulb and the wet-bulb, works out the dry-air mass flow G, and sets L/G = water flow ÷ G. It also shows the enthalpy of the air leaving the fill and the saturated temperature that equals; this should be a few kelvin below the water coming in. The air flow is entered once, at the design point. For another fan speed, use the fan percentage in section 3: the air is scaled in proportion to speed.

          What is assumed

          The characteristic keeps the same constant and slope at other flows. The air flow of the first case is fixed unless the fan percentage is changed; it is not recalculated for the higher fill pressure drop at a higher flow, which the handbook example also ignores. Water is 1 kg per litre with cw = 4.186 kJ/(kg·K). Hot-air recirculation, wet-bulb rise at the tower inlet and bypass wall water (handbook section 16) are not modelled.

          How much does the assumed L/G or slope matter? (option 4)

          Little for a flow change at constant load. Varying the assumed L/G from 0.7 to 1.8 moves the new water-out temperature by about 0.1 to 0.3 K; the slope m matters about as much. The sheet prints the spread under the result: for L/G 0.7 to 1.6 and m 0.5 to 0.8 when L/G is assumed, or for m 0.5 to 0.8 when L/G comes from the air flow. For a firm answer use the maker’s characteristic or performance curve at the new flow.

          Approach limit

          The handbook notes that makers rarely guarantee less than about 2.8 K approach. The sheet marks anything below that.

          Water use

          Three evaporation estimates are shown side by side. They normally agree within about 15 %. The empirical formula and the heat balance (at about 89 % evaporative share) are the same line; the rule of thumb of 0.1 % per °F is the more conservative. Bleed-off is set by the cycles of concentration: B = E/(N − 1). Make-up is evaporation plus bleed-off plus drift. Cycles of concentration depend on the make-up water quality and treatment, and the conductivity ratio gives it directly where you can measure both waters. The sheet does not model the variation of evaporation with the dry-bulb temperature and humidity of the air, nor water used for filter backwash or basin overflow.

          Drift loss: what is acceptable

          Drift is water carried out as droplets, as a percentage of the circulating flow. In normal design:

          • 0.002 % or less: good, and the usual specification for a new tower with high-efficiency drift eliminators. This is the current Eurovent certification threshold.
          • 0.005 %: a common maker’s guarantee for conventional eliminators.
          • 0.01 %: the upper limit of acceptable practice today. It was the previous Eurovent threshold.
          • 0.02 %: the older specification quoted in M374 §10, now regarded as poor. Modern eliminators reach 0.001 to 0.003 %.

          Drift matters for water loss (small) and for Legionella control and nearby plant, where the droplets can carry bacteria and chemicals off the site. The figure is a maker’s guarantee, tested to a standard such as CTI ATC-140 or the Eurovent programme, and holds only while the eliminators are clean and undamaged and the tower runs near its rated air velocity. The sheet marks anything above 0.01 % as high. Check the limit in your project specification or local code: the Hong Kong EMSD code of practice for water-cooled air-conditioning systems gives no numeric drift limit, but requires eliminators to be maintained, and sets a minimum of 6 cycles of concentration for fresh water and 2 for sea water (section 2.4.2).
