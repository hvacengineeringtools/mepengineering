            Auto and Manual mode

            • Auto keeps the four boxes consistent. You give three, the sheet gives the fourth. Use it to find a size or a flow.
            • Manual changes nothing you type. The pressure drop uses the flow, width and depth you entered. The velocity box is optional and is only compared with the velocity those three give; a warning shows if they differ by more than 2 %. Use it to check a size already chosen on a drawing or schedule.
            • Velocity is not an independent input to the friction: it follows from flow and size. In Manual mode a velocity that disagrees is therefore flagged, never used.
            • Switching from Manual to Auto keeps your figures. If all four boxes are full, the one you typed longest ago becomes the calculated one. Switching from Auto to Manual leaves the shaded box as an ordinary figure you can edit.

            What the sheet does

            It finds the pressure lost to friction in a straight length of rectangular duct. The duct is turned into a round duct that loses the same pressure, and the Darcy–Weisbach equation is used with the Altshul–Tsal friction factor. Any three of flow, velocity, width and depth fix the fourth. Section 2 turns this round: it gives the sizes that meet your limits.

            Choices left to you

            • The spare capacity, air temperature, roughness, velocity limit and friction limit are yours. Our reference sheet adds 15 % to the flow, uses a roughness of 0.15 mm and takes air at 1.2 kg/m³. This sheet starts with no spare capacity; type 15 to match the reference sheet.
            • Why the figures run 2 to 3 % below the reference sheet. The reference sheet works out the Reynolds number as velocity × diameter ÷ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, but 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s is the dynamic viscosity of air. The Reynolds number needs the kinematic viscosity, which is the dynamic viscosity divided by the density (about 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s at 20 °C). This sheet uses the kinematic value, so its Reynolds number is higher and its pressure drop is lower: 3.0 % lower for 150 × 150 mm at 0.05 m³/s, 1.6 % for 500 × 400 mm at 1 m³/s, and 1.8 % for 1600 × 1000 mm at 8 m³/s. If you need to reproduce the reference sheet exactly, treat its figures as the slightly higher, more cautious ones.
            • Velocity can mean the flow over the real width × depth area, or over the area of the equivalent round duct, which is what our reference sheet does. The friction always uses the equivalent round duct velocity. The two velocities differ by about 5 % for a square duct and more for a flat one.

            Equivalent round duct

            • The sheet uses D = 1.265 ( (a b)³ / (a + b) )^0.2, the form in our reference sheet, which it calls the hydraulic diameter. It is not 4A/P. The ASHRAE Handbook prints the Huebscher form D = 1.30 (a b)^0.625 / (a + b)^0.25, which is a little different;
            • The equivalent diameter gives the same friction at the same flow. It does not give the same velocity.
            • Very flat ducts (width to depth above about 4) are costlier and the equivalent diameter is less reliable. The sheet warns above the ratio you set in section 3.

            Circular duct

            • Section 2 uses the same method as section 1 (Darcy–Weisbach with the Altshul–Tsal friction factor) but with the real diameter, so no equivalent diameter is involved. The velocity is the flow over the real round area.
            • Air temperature, spare capacity, roughness and the two limits are shared with section 1. Change them there. The flow box in section 2 is its own.
            • The table of round sizes lists standard diameters, smallest first, that meet your velocity and friction limits at the flow. The smallest is shown in bold. Click a row to put that diameter into the calculator.
            • Spiral and flexible round ducts have their own roughness: choose the material in section 1. A flexible duct is only good for this method when it is fully stretched.

            What the friction formula covers

            • Altshul–Tsal is an explicit approximation to the Colebrook equation for turbulent flow. The sheet treats it as valid from Reynolds number 4 000 up to 10⁸ and relative roughness up to 0.05. Duct flows are usually well inside this.
            • Roughness values are typical figures, not from a particular clause. Use the maker’s or your specification’s value where there is one.

            What is not included

            Bends, tees, transitions, dampers, grilles, louvres, coils, filters, attenuators, leakage and duct ageing. Pressure drop across these items is added separately. Air is taken at sea-level pressure.
